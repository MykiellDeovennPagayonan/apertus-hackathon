# Nitrogen cycle

Date: 2026-10-10  
Status: Proposed simulation concept.

## Concept

Why can a plant lack nitrogen when the air around it contains abundant nitrogen gas?

The simulation shows a planted landscape with a soil cutaway, roots, decomposers, and microbial process markers. Learners follow nitrogen among atmospheric nitrogen gas, soil ammonium, soil nitrate, plants, animals, and dead organic material. Labels and shapes distinguish chemical forms so the experience does not treat all nitrogen as one immediately usable resource.

Begin with a growing plant and trace how nitrogen becomes available to it. Nitrogen fixation converts atmospheric nitrogen into biologically available forms; selected microbes carry out this process. A legume scenario shows nitrogen-fixing microbes associated with root nodules rather than implying that every plant can directly use atmospheric nitrogen gas. Plant uptake and assimilation incorporate available nitrogen into organic matter, feeding transfers it to animals, and decomposition returns organic nitrogen to inorganic forms through mineralization.

The soil view makes microbial transformations visible. Nitrification converts ammonium through nitrite to nitrate under suitable oxygen conditions. Denitrification can return nitrate-derived nitrogen to the atmosphere under low-oxygen conditions. These are distinct pathways with conditions and delays, rather than mandatory stages that every nitrogen atom completes in order.

Learners compare an untreated plot, a plot with a nitrogen-fixing crop, and a plot receiving a bounded fertilizer input. Rainfall can move nitrate out of the root zone or transport nitrogen toward a pond. A linked pond scenario can show increased algal growth when nitrogen is limiting, followed by oxygen demand as organic material decomposes. The lesson should connect nutrient availability to ecosystem effects without implying that nitrogen is always the limiting nutrient.

## Core investigations

1. Follow nitrogen from the atmosphere through fixation, plant uptake, feeding, and decomposition.
2. Compare a plant with and without nitrogen-fixing microbial partners under otherwise identical conditions.
3. Add nitrogen fertilizer and observe how the growth response changes as another resource becomes limiting.
4. Apply the same rainfall to different nitrogen-input scenarios and compare plant uptake with nitrogen losses.
5. Compare aerated and waterlogged soil and inspect the changes in microbial pathways.

## Learning goals

- Distinguish atmospheric nitrogen gas from forms available for plant uptake.
- Identify fixation, assimilation, mineralization, nitrification, and denitrification.
- Explain the role of microbes in transforming nitrogen.
- Trace nitrogen through living organisms, dead material, soil, water, and the atmosphere.
- Recognize that added nutrients can leave the intended system and affect nearby ecosystems.

## Model boundaries

Track nitrogen amounts on a common elemental-nitrogen basis across chemical forms. Every transfer must have a source and destination; fertilizer inputs, atmospheric exchanges, leaching, and runoff must be recorded explicitly. Keep stores nonnegative and distinguish plant uptake from microbial conversion. This is a simplified teaching network, not a complete account of nitrogen chemistry or guidance for fertilizer application. Optional links to the nutrient-limitation and oxygen-cycle concepts must use declared transfers and shared units.
