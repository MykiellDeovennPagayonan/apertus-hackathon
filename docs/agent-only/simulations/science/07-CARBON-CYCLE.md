# Carbon cycle

Date: 2026-10-10  
Status: Proposed simulation concept.

## Concept

How can the same carbon move through air, organisms, soil, oceans, and rocks on very different timescales?

The simulation presents a connected landscape containing vegetation, animals, soil, an ocean, and a geological cutaway. Learners select a carbon tracer and follow possible routes among atmospheric carbon dioxide, living biomass, dead organic matter, soil carbon, ocean carbon, and long-term geological stores. Each arrow names the process responsible for the transfer.

The first experience follows the faster biological pathways. Photosynthesis transfers carbon from carbon dioxide into organic matter, feeding moves it among organisms, and respiration and decomposition return some carbon to the atmosphere. Some dead material remains in soil stores for longer periods. Plants both take up carbon through photosynthesis and release carbon through respiration; the balance determines their modeled net carbon gain.

A timescale switch introduces slower pathways, including burial, formation of long-term geological stores, weathering, and exchanges involving ocean sediments and rocks. These pathways should use separate teaching presets rather than making fossil fuels appear after a few ordinary growing seasons. Carbon can remain in a store for long periods, and it does not have to visit every store in a fixed loop.

Learners then compare land clearing, regrowth, and fossil-fuel combustion. Clearing transfers carbon according to the selected scenario: some may be released by burning, some may enter dead material, and some may remain in harvested material. Regrowth removes carbon from the atmosphere gradually as biomass accumulates. Fossil-fuel combustion transfers carbon from a long-term store to the atmosphere much faster than that store formed.

An ocean view shows exchange between air and water and storage as dissolved inorganic carbon, rather than treating ocean carbon as only dissolved carbon dioxide. The main observation is the difference between the size of a carbon store and the rate of transfer: a large store is not necessarily a rapid sink, and a small imbalance between inputs and outputs can change atmospheric carbon over time.

## Core investigations

1. Trace carbon through photosynthesis, feeding, respiration, and decomposition.
2. Compare an intact forest with a cleared area and inspect where the displaced carbon goes.
3. Restore vegetation and compare the pace of carbon uptake with an earlier release.
4. Add fossil-fuel combustion and follow the transfer from geological storage to the atmosphere.
5. Switch timescales and compare biological cycling with long-term burial and geological pathways.
6. Inspect ocean exchange and compare carbon stored with carbon transferred per model step.

## Learning goals

- Distinguish carbon stores from the processes that transfer carbon between them.
- Explain how photosynthesis, respiration, feeding, and decomposition connect organisms to atmospheric carbon.
- Compare fast biological pathways with slow geological pathways.
- Recognize that combustion redistributes existing carbon rather than creating it.
- Explain why regrowth and ocean uptake do not automatically offset an arbitrary release immediately.
- Distinguish carbon cycling from energy flow through an ecosystem.

## Model boundaries

Use a common elemental-carbon unit for all stores and transfers; clearly distinguish carbon mass from carbon dioxide mass if both are displayed. Preserve carbon balance, limit transfers to available material, and record any inputs or exports across the scene boundary. State which geological processes are schematic and which timescales are compressed. The first version teaches carbon movement; a climate or ocean-acidification extension requires an explicit additional model rather than deriving temperature or pH directly from a decorative arrow.
