# Food-web dynamics

Date: 2026-10-10  
Status: Proposed simulation concept informed by ecological research.

## Concept

How can a change to one population affect organisms that it neither eats nor is eaten by?

The simulation presents a bounded ecosystem containing producers, several consumers, predators, omnivores, and decomposers. Feeding links form a web rather than a single chain. Arrows show the direction of energy transfer from a consumed organism to its consumer, while the living scene shows populations using shared resources and responding over time.

Learners begin with a stable fictional ecosystem and trace energy from producers through multiple pathways. They can then change one pressure—such as reducing a predator, lowering one prey population, changing producer productivity, or removing one feeding link—and compare the resulting population histories with the unchanged ecosystem.

The most important observation is the difference between **direct effects** and **indirect effects**. Reducing a predator can directly release its prey from predation; increased prey may then reduce producers, affecting other consumers that share those producers. A population can therefore change even when it has no direct link to the original disturbance. These connected changes are possible trophic cascades, but the simulation should not imply that every disturbance produces a simple or predictable cascade.

The food web should include alternative energy pathways. A consumer with several prey sources may switch resources when one declines, whereas a specialist may be more vulnerable. Removing apparently redundant species may have little immediate effect but can leave fewer alternative pathways, making the web more vulnerable to a later disturbance. Omnivory and competition also mean that organisms do not always fit into perfectly separate trophic levels.

Decomposition must remain part of the system. Dead material and waste enter a detrital pathway; decomposers recycle nutrients that support producers. Energy flows through the web and is dissipated, while matter is recycled. Population counts, biomass, and energy are related measurements but should not be treated as interchangeable.

## Core investigations

1. Remove or reduce a top predator and trace direct and indirect population changes.
2. Reduce a producer and observe how a bottom-up disturbance travels through consumers.
3. Compare a specialist consumer with a generalist that has alternative prey.
4. Remove one of two species with similar feeding roles, then apply a second disturbance.
5. Compare a simple food chain with a connected food web containing alternative pathways.

## Learning goals

- Read food-web arrows as energy moving from resource or prey to consumer.
- Distinguish producers, consumers, omnivores, predators, and decomposers.
- Trace direct effects separately from indirect effects.
- Explain top-down and bottom-up changes without assuming every response has the same direction or strength.
- Recognize that biodiversity includes interaction pathways, not only a count of species.
- Treat the model as a simplified ecosystem scenario rather than a forecast for a real habitat.

## Research basis

NOAA describes food webs as representations of feeding relationships and energy flow, and emphasizes that removing predators or adding nutrients can cause direct and cascading indirect effects. USGS similarly identifies indirect interactions, keystone predators, and trophic cascades as central properties studied through empirical work and mathematical models.

Experimental research shows why the simulation should preserve interaction strength and alternative pathways. Removing strongly interacting consumers can cause large cascades, but loss of weak interactors can also reduce ecosystem stability. Other experiments indicate that trophic redundancy can reduce vulnerability to secondary extinctions. Research on trophic coherence further shows that stability cannot be inferred from the number of species or links alone; how feeding relationships are organized also matters.
